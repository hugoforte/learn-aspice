AI-generated work products under assessment

When agents draft the requirements, the code and the tests, what does an assessor look at? The VDA Guidelines answer directly, and the answer is mostly "the same things, plus configuration management of the AI itself".

Seat-Heat adopts coding agents

Agents draft software requirements from customer specifications, write code, and generate unit and HIL test specifications. Engineers review each agent pull request before merging. Which model, version and settings produced a change is not recorded; prompts live in engineers' chat histories. At the end of the release, an agent writes the missing design documentation from the code.

What the Guidelines say

The Guidelines' section on AI assistance starts from the WHAT/HOW split: "there is no limitation or recommendation regarding tool usage", and using an AI tool "is a how-level decision". The assessor still rates the WHAT[Guidelines §2.5.5, p. 98]. Their worked example: a developer generates code with an AI tool, reviews it against the detailed design and the coding guidelines, and corrects it. The assessor spot-checks, and "if no deviations are found, there is no negative impact on the rating of process attribute PA1.1 of the SWE.3 process"[p. 98].

Then the warning: AI tools produce "less accurate and sometimes even unusable results … just as often as good ones", so "the assessed organization shall ensure with clear criteria that risks from AI engineering tools will be adequately handled", whether through review, test-driven development or another approach[p. 99]. Among the places it may impact, the Guidelines name SUP.8, SUP.1 and PA 2.2, with examples[p. 99]:

  • new configuration items, such as the prompts used to generate source code;
  • a maintained list of development tools, including approval of specific AI tools;
  • version control of "AI tools, models, and settings, because their behavior changes over time";
  • input and output work products of AI assistance under quality control.

Four consequences worth designing for

ConcernWhere ASPICE puts it
ReproducibilityModel, version, settings and prompt as configuration items: SUP.8.BP1–BP4, GP 2.2.3.
Clear criteria for AI outputReview and quality criteria (GP 2.2.1), reviews against them (GP 2.2.4); SUP.1.BP2–BP3.
Who may approveASPICE doesn't name a role. It asks that responsibilities and authorities are defined (GP 2.1.3). For changes, the Guidelines expect the approving body to represent all required stakeholders, and its participants to "have the necessary authority to take decisions"[§3.26.2.3, p. 209].
Evidence written after the factThe plausibility check: documents made shortly before an assessment are not considered, and histories should match the work's rhythm[§1.4.3.2, p. 38]. AI makes it cheap to generate plausible documents late, and an assessor looking at the history will notice.

Where self-review stops counting. GP 2.2.4 asks for reviews against criteria; its text doesn't demand independent reviewers. Independence is SUP.1's requirement, "no self-monitoring"[p. 79], and the Guidelines' SUP.1 section links it to "the four-eyes principle, such as self-monitoring of the author of a work product is not sufficient"[§3.23, p. 192]. So an agent checking its own output can support a review, but it can never be your quality assurance. Having a different reviewer from the producer is the safe design.
