Seat-Heat adopts coding agents

Agents draft software requirements from customer specifications, write code, and generate unit and HIL test specifications. Engineers review each agent pull request before merging. Which model, version and settings produced a change is not recorded; prompts live in engineers' chat histories. At the end of the release, an agent writes the missing design documentation from the code.

What the Guidelines say

The Guidelines' section on AI assistance starts from the WHAT/HOW split: "there is no limitation or recommendation regarding tool usage", and using an AI tool "is a how-level decision". The assessor still rates the WHAT[Guidelines §2.5.5, p. 98]. Their worked example: a developer generates code with an AI tool, reviews it against the detailed design and the coding guidelines, and corrects it. The assessor spot-checks, and "if no deviations are found, there is no negative impact on the rating"[p. 98].

Then the warning: AI tools produce "less accurate and sometimes even unusable results … just as often as good ones", so "the assessed organization shall ensure with clear criteria that risks from AI engineering tools will be adequately handled", whether through review, test-driven development or another approach[p. 99]. The Guidelines name SUP.8, SUP.1 and PA 2.2 as the places this reaches, with examples[p. 99]:

  • new configuration items, such as the prompts used to generate source code;
  • a maintained list of development tools, including approval of specific AI tools;
  • version control of "AI tools, models, and settings, because their behavior changes over time";
  • input and output work products of AI assistance under quality control.

Four consequences worth designing for

ConcernWhere ASPICE puts it
ReproducibilityModel, version, settings and prompt as configuration items: SUP.8.BP1–BP4, GP 2.2.3.
Clear criteria for AI outputReview and quality criteria (GP 2.2.1), reviews against them (GP 2.2.4); SUP.1.BP2–BP3.
Who may approveASPICE doesn't name a role. It asks that responsibilities and authorities are defined (GP 2.1.3). For changes, the Guidelines expect the approving body to include all relevant stakeholders "with the necessary authority to take decisions"[§3.26.2.3, p. 209].
Evidence written after the factThe plausibility check: documents made shortly before an assessment are not considered, and histories should match the work's rhythm[§1.4.3.2, p. 38]. AI makes it cheap to generate plausible documents late, and an assessor looking at the history will notice.

Self-review is a design question, not an ASPICE rule. GP 2.2.4 asks for reviews against criteria, not independent ones. Only SUP.1 requires independence, "no self-monitoring"[p. 79]. An agent that checks its own output against the criteria is doing verification, not quality assurance, and it cannot stand in for SUP.1. Making the reviewer differ from the producer is sound practice. Present it as your choice, not as ASPICE's demand.

Can an agent's self-check of its own output count as SUP.1 quality assurance?

List three things Seat-Heat should put under configuration management now that agents write code.

The prompts (or task instructions) that generate work products; the AI tools, models and their versions and settings; and the approved-tool list. Also keep the inputs and outputs of AI assistance under quality control.

Your win: you can set up AI-assisted development so its output is assessable, and you know which of your safeguards are ASPICE demands and which are your own choices.
