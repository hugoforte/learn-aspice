Lesson 1 · Automotive SPICE through rig

The two axes of a rating

You cannot ask "what level is rig?" By the end of this lesson you will know what to ask instead, and you will be able to turn a row of ratings into a capability level in your head.

A grid, not a score

An Automotive SPICE assessment does not give an organisation one number. It fills in a grid with two axes[PAM §3, p. 13]:

  • The process dimension: which processes are being looked at. These come from the process reference model (PRM): 30-odd named processes such as MAN.3 Project Management, SUP.8 Configuration Management or SUP.10 Change Request Management. Each one has a purpose and a list of outcomes, the observable results of achieving that purpose[p. 14].
  • The capability dimension: how well each process is run, as a capability level from 0 to 5. Each level is made up of process attributes (PAs), and each PA gets rated on its own[§3.2.1, p. 17].

The process assessment model (PAM) is the PRM plus indicators, meaning practices to look for and information items to look for. An assessor uses them to gather objective evidence and justify each rating[§3.3, p. 23]. So the output is a capability profile: one level per assessed process.

For rig, this changes the question. Asking "what level is rig?" has no answer. The answerable question is "when a team works through rig, what level does its project management reach? And its configuration management?" One rig can come out at level 2 on one process and level 0 on another.

The PAM is a WHAT; rig is a HOW

The PAM describes three levels of abstraction for the word "process"[§3.3.3, Fig. 3, p. 26]:

LevelWhat it holdsIn rig's world
WHATGoals of the process, why they matter, dependencies. This is where the PAM sits."Progress of the project is monitored and reported" (MAN.3 outcome 6)
HOWMethods, tools, templates, workflows, who is allowed to do what.rig itself: the phase model, the gates, AGENTS.md's rules, DESIGN.md
DOINGPerforming the tasks with the method on a real piece of work.One work: its work.json, its context.md, its PRs

The PAM deliberately prescribes no lifecycle, no order and no document structure[§3.3.4, pp. 26–27]. Mapping a HOW such as rig onto the PAM's indicators is the assessor's job: they read the defined HOW, interview people about the DOING, inspect repositories, then rate. The rest of this course practises that mapping on rig.

The one rule that turns ratings into a level

Level n is reached when every attribute of level n is rated L or F, and every attribute below level n is rated F.[§3.2.4, Table 9, p. 22]

"Largely" is good enough for the top level you claim but not for the levels underneath it. A process with PA 1.1 at L can never be level 2, however well it is managed: the foundation is still missing some of its outcomes.

C. From memory

Close the page above in your mind. State the rule that turns PA ratings into a capability level.

Level n requires every PA of level n rated L or F, and every PA of the levels below rated F.

A colleague asks, "So what ASPICE level is rig?" In two sentences, what do you answer?

ASPICE rates processes (say MAN.3 or SUP.8), not tools, and gives each one its own level. rig is a HOW: an assessor would map what rig does, and the records the works left behind, onto each process's indicators and rate the processes it covers.

Your win: you can read a capability profile and check that it adds up, and you know that rating rig means choosing processes first. Next lesson: read one process, MAN.3 Project Management, and hold its outcomes against a real rig work. That is PA 1.1, the foundation every higher level depends on.
