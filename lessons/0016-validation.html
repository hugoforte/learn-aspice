Validation: VAL.1

Every process on the right of the V asks whether the product matches its specification. VAL.1 asks a different question: does the end product do what its users actually need, where they actually use it?

A test checks that two software components exchange data within their specified timing. Which process?

The complete ECU is checked against its heat-up requirement in a climate chamber. Which process?

Verification and validation are different questions

VAL.1's purpose is to provide evidence that "the end product, allowing direct end user interaction, satisfies the intended use expectations in its operational target environment"[PAM §4.5.1, p. 58]. Verification checks against a specification; validation checks against intended use.

The Guidelines' own example is an automatic transmission. Meeting the specified gear-shift times is verification, "given that these can be measured objectively". A good gear-shift feel is validation, "as it requires feedback from end users or their representatives"[Guidelines §3.14.1, p. 163]. Without legal requirements behind them, validation targets may be "exploratory, or even subjective".

So validation measures look different from verification measures. The PAM's examples: operational use-case testing under real-life conditions, highly accelerated life testing, simulations under real-life conditions, end-user trials, panel or blind tests, and expert panels[PAM terminology, p. 10].

Who validates

Because VAL.1 is about end users, the Guidelines say it "excludes looking at pure embedded software products, an ECU, or a drive": none of them "provides a direct end user interface"[Guidelines §3.14.1, p. 163].

For Seat-Heat this settles it. Its software, and even the whole seat-heating ECU from lesson 14, has no direct end-user interface. Whether level 2 feels warm enough on a winter morning is a question about the seat in the car, and it belongs to whoever delivers that product. The sources don't say whether that is the seat maker or the vehicle maker. What Seat-Heat delivers is evidenced by verification: SWE.4–SWE.6, and SYS.4–SYS.5 for the ECU.

VAL.1 is a flex process: outside the base of the recommended VDA Scope, added when the assessment purpose needs it[Guidelines Fig. 1-1, p. 29]. In practice, reading this with the exclusion above, that means an organisation delivering an end product that people use directly (the course's inference).

What VAL.1 asks for

Its five base practices follow lesson 14's pattern, with validation measures in place of verification measures[PAM pp. 58–59]:

  1. Specify validation measures for the end product, based on the stakeholder requirements, to show it fulfils its intended use in its operational target environment.
  2. Select them, with criteria including regression, and sufficient coverage for the release scope. Criteria include the release's purpose: test bench, test track, public road, field use by end users.
  3. Perform the validation of the integrated end product, record the results with pass/fail status, and evaluate them. Deviations go to SUP.9.
  4. Trace validation measures to the stakeholder requirements they derive from, "if applicable", and results to measures.
  5. Summarise and communicate the results.
  • One-way tracing can be enough. When a validation measure comes from a legal or homologation requirement, bidirectional traceability to that source is not possible, and "unidirectional traceability is sufficient"[VAL.1.BP4 note 8, p. 59].
  • Validation feeds requirements back. Results can identify new stakeholder or system requirements, for example from mock-ups or concept studies (BP3 note 5). Where needs are unclear or change often, repeated validation of increments helps refine them (BP1 note 2).
  • Similar rating rules to verification, though with no rule on automated scripts. Entry criteria may be defined per set of measures (VAL.1.RL.1). Exploratory validation needn't trace to requirements (the second rule, which the Guidelines misprint as VAL.4.RL.2). A sound selection against a poor release plan is not downrated (VAL.1.RL.3)[Guidelines §3.14.2–3.14.3, pp. 164–165].

A. Verification or validation?

The seat reaches 35 °C within 4 minutes at −20 °C, measured in a climate chamber.

A panel of drivers rates whether the heat-up feels fast enough on a cold morning.

A test confirms that heating switches off after 60 minutes, as the requirement specifies.

B. Scope and traceability

Should Seat-Heat's assessment of its ECU include VAL.1?

A validation measure is derived from a homologation requirement. Must the trace to that requirement be bidirectional?

Trials show occupants want level 1 to be warmer. Where does that finding go next?

C. From memory

In one sentence each: what does verification check, and what does validation check?

Verification checks a work product or the product against its specification (requirements, architecture, design). Validation checks that the end product meets its users' intended-use expectations in its real operating environment.

Why does an ECU supplier usually not perform VAL.1?

VAL.1 is about intended use by end users, and the Guidelines exclude pure embedded software, ECUs and drives because none has a direct end-user interface. The supplier's evidence is verification; validation belongs to whoever delivers the end product.
